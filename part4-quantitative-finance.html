    4.1 Pricing derivatives

    Concept primer

    Derivative pricing rests on one principle: no arbitrage. If a portfolio's payoff can be copied exactly by trading the underlying asset and cash, the derivative must cost the same as that replicating portfolio, otherwise someone earns a riskless profit.

    Instruments. A call option gives the right (not the obligation) to buy the asset at the strike KKK at maturity T, so it pays max⁡(ST−K,0)\max (S_{T} - K, 0)max(ST​−K,0); a put pays max⁡(K−ST,0)\max (K - S_{T}, 0)max(K−ST​,0). European options can be exercised only at maturity, American options any time before. A forward obliges both sides to trade at a fixed price. Options are valuable because the loss is limited to the premium while the gain is not.

    Risk-neutral pricing. In a model where replication is possible, there is a probability measure (the risk-neutral measure) under which the discounted asset price is a martingale; the price of any payoff is its expectation under that measure, discounted at the risk-free rate. These are not real-world probabilities; they are a bookkeeping device that encodes the no-arbitrage prices. In a one-period binomial model with up factor uuu, down factor ddd and gross rate RRR, the risk-neutral up probability is q=R−du−dq = \frac{R - d}{u - d}q=u−dR−d​, which must lie between 0 and 1 (that condition is exactly no-arbitrage: d<R<ud < R < ud<R<u).

    Black-Scholes. In continuous time the stock follows geometric Brownian motion dS=μSdt+σSdWdS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dWdS=μSdt+σSdW. Hedging an option continuously with δ\delta δ shares removes the randomness, so the option's value solves a partial differential equation in which the stock's drift μ\mu μ does not appear. The solution for a European call is the formula in Q3. The inputs are the stock price, strike, time to maturity, interest rate and volatility; volatility is the only input not directly observed, so markets quote options by their implied volatility.

    Greeks. The sensitivities of the option value: δ\delta δ (to the stock price), γ\gamma γ (change in δ\delta δ), vega (volatility), θ\theta θ (time), ρ\rho ρ (interest rate). Traders hedge by making δ\delta δ zero and monitor γ\gamma γ and vega.

    Limits of the model. Real prices jump, volatility changes with time and strike (the volatility smile), trading has costs, and hedging is discrete. Stochastic volatility, jump and local-volatility models address these.

    4.2 Portfolios and risk

    Concept primer

    Portfolio theory answers: how should money be split across risky assets, and how do we measure the risk left?

    Return and risk. A portfolio's expected return is the weighted average of the assets' expected returns. Its variance involves covariances, so combining assets that do not move together lowers risk below the average of the individual risks; this is diversification. Risk that cannot be diversified away is market (systematic) risk. The Sharpe ratio, excess return divided by volatility, measures return per unit of risk. Mean-variance optimization (Markowitz) chooses weights to minimize variance for a target return; its output is very sensitive to estimation errors in the inputs, which is why practitioners shrink estimates or add constraints.

    Risk measures. Volatility treats gains and losses alike. Value at Risk (VaR) at level 95% is the loss that is exceeded only 5% of the time; it says nothing about how bad the tail is. Conditional VaR (expected shortfall) is the average loss in that worst 5%. A coherent risk measure satisfies monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity and subadditivity (merging portfolios never increases risk); VaR can violate subadditivity, CVaR cannot.

    Dynamic allocation. Merton's problem chooses, continuously, how much to hold in a risky asset to maximize expected utility of final wealth. For constant relative risk aversion the optimal risky fraction is constant: (excess return) / (risk aversion x variance). The Kelly criterion maximizes the long-run growth rate of wealth and corresponds to logarithmic utility; it bets more aggressively than most investors can tolerate, so half Kelly is common.

    Risk-sensitive decisions. Standard reinforcement learning maximizes expected reward. Finance cares about the whole distribution; replacing the expectation by a risk measure makes the problem harder because it breaks the simple Bellman recursion.

    Q9. Define Value at Risk (VaR) and Conditional VaR (CVaR). Why is VaR not a coherent risk measure?

    Answer. The 95% VaR of a loss is the level exceeded only 5% of the time (the 95th percentile of the loss). CVaR (expected shortfall) is the average loss given that the loss exceeds VaR. For losses that are normal with mean 0 and standard deviation 1: VaR at 95%=1.64595\% = 1.64595%=1.645 and CVaR = 2.063. A coherent risk measure satisfies monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity and subadditivity (the risk of a combination is no more than the sum of the risks, so diversification never looks bad). VaR can fail subadditivity: two independent bonds each lose 100 with probability 4%; each has 95% VaR of 0, but the pair loses at least 100 with probability 1−0.962=7.8%>5%1 - 0.96^{2} = 7.8\% > 5\%1−0.962=7.8%>5%, so the pair's 95% VaR is 100>0+0100 > 0 + 0100>0+0. CVaR is coherent and, as shown by Rockafellar and Uryasev, can be minimized by convex (linear-program) methods, which is why it is used in optimization.

    Q11. Risk-sensitive objectives in reinforcement learning: why are they harder?

    Answer. Standard RL maximizes the expected total reward, ignoring risk. Replacing it by a risk measure such as CVaR, variance-penalized reward, or entropic risk (the logarithm of the expected exponential of the reward) breaks the usual Bellman recursion: the optimal action at a later time may depend on what happened earlier (time inconsistency), so the policy is not a function of the current state alone. Remedies: augment the state with a running quantity (the accumulated reward or a risk budget), use the entropic risk whose recursion is multiplicative and stays Markov, or optimize the policy directly by gradients on the risk objective.

    4.3 Trading, execution and pricing decisions

    Concept primer

    Here the question changes from "what is it worth?" to "how do I trade or price it well?". The common thread: decisions are sequential, the market reacts to your actions, and you must trade off cost, risk and information.

    Market microstructure. Exchanges match orders in a limit order book: resting buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks) at each price. The spread is the gap between the best bid and ask; a market order pays it, a limit order may earn it but may not be filled. Large orders move prices (market impact) because they consume depth; part of the impact fades (temporary) and part persists (permanent, information).

    Execution. Splitting a large order over time reduces impact but exposes you to price risk while you wait. The Almgren-Chriss framework makes this a clean optimization: with impact cost quadratic in the trading rate, a risk-neutral trader sells at a constant rate; risk aversion front-loads the schedule. Benchmarks used in practice: arrival price, TWAP (time-weighted average price) and VWAP (volume-weighted average price).

    Market making. A market maker posts both bid and ask, profits from the spread, and suffers when prices move while holding inventory or when informed traders hit the quotes. Models set quotes around a reservation price shifted by inventory, so the maker leans against its position.

    Dynamic pricing. A seller chooses prices over time to maximize revenue. With known linear demand the optimal price is simple; with unknown demand the seller must learn while earning, which is a bandit problem; with limited stock and a deadline it becomes a dynamic program in which the price depends on the time and stock left.

    Why these are research problems. Each combines modelling (how prices or demand respond), optimization (what policy is best) and learning (what happens when the model is unknown), the exact mix this group works on.

    Q12. Explain the limit order book, spread and market impact.

    Answer. An exchange keeps a list of buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks) with price and size; the best bid and best ask define the spread, and the mid-price is their average. A market order trades immediately at the best available prices, paying the spread and consuming depth (a large order walks up the book, giving market impact: the price moves against the trader, with a temporary part that fades and a permanent part that reflects information). A limit order rests in the book at a chosen price and trades only if the market comes to it; it earns the spread but risks adverse selection (being filled mostly when the price is about to move against you). Orders at the same price are served first-in first-out (price-time priority).

    Q14. Market making and inventory risk.

    Answer. A market maker quotes a bid and an ask and earns the spread when both sides trade, but accumulates inventory that is exposed to price moves. In the Avellaneda-Stoikov model the maker's reservation price is the mid-price minus (inventory x risk aversion x variance x time remaining), so a long inventory lowers both quotes (to sell more and buy less), and the quotes are centred on that price with a spread that depends on how fast orders arrive when quotes move away from the mid. Adverse selection (trades by informed traders) is the main cost: quote wider when you suspect informed flow.

    4.4 Quantitative modelling and evaluation

    Concept primer

    Modelling data from markets is full of traps; this section is about being honest with data.

    Time series. Prices are usually non-stationary (their statistical behaviour changes), so analysts work with returns. Autoregressive models predict a value from recent values; mean-reverting processes such as Ornstein-Uhlenbeck pull toward a long-run level; cointegration describes two non-stationary series with a stationary combination (the basis of pairs trading); GARCH models let volatility cluster in time.

    Backtesting. A backtest simulates a strategy on past data. Typical sources of false confidence: look-ahead bias (using information that was not available at the time), survivorship bias (including only assets that survived), overfitting through many trials (a best-of-1000 result is partly luck; the deflated Sharpe ratio corrects for this), ignoring costs and market impact, and testing on data used to design the strategy. A trustworthy workflow: fix the rules before looking at the test data, use walk-forward evaluation, include realistic costs, report risk and drawdowns, and confirm with paper trading.

    Communicating results. State the claim, the baseline, how many things you tried, and what could make the result disappear. A modest result with an honest test is worth more than a spectacular but fragile one.

    Q17. How would you test a trading strategy honestly?

    Answer. Split time so the test period is after the training period; use walk-forward testing; include realistic costs (spreads, fees, market impact) and delays; avoid look-ahead bias (using information not available at decision time, such as revised data) and survivorship bias (using only assets that still exist); count how many strategies or parameters were tried, since the best of many random strategies looks good by chance (multiple testing; the deflated Sharpe ratio adjusts for this); report drawdowns and tail risk, not only the Sharpe ratio; compare with a simple baseline; and verify in paper trading before risking capital.
