    2.1 Probability and statistics

    Concept primer

    Probability is the most frequently tested mathematics in this area. The skill is turning a story into events and then using a small set of rules.

    Foundations. A sample space lists all outcomes; an event is a set of outcomes; probabilities are between 0 and 1 and add over disjoint events. Conditional probability: P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)P(B)P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​. Events are independent if P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). The law of total probability splits an event over a partition: P(A)P(A)P(A) = sum over i of P(A∣Bi)P(Bi)P(A \mid B_{i}) P(B_{i})P(A∣Bi​)P(Bi​). Bayes' rule reverses a conditioning: P(B∣A)=P(A∣B)P(B)P(A)P(B \mid A) = P(A \mid B) \frac{P(B)}{P(A)}P(B∣A)=P(A∣B)P(A)P(B)​. The standard trap is the base-rate fallacy: a test that is 99% accurate for a disease that affects 1 in 1000 people gives a positive result that is mostly false, because the many healthy people produce more false positives than the few sick ones produce true positives.

    Random variables. A random variable maps outcomes to numbers. The expectation is the probability-weighted average, E[X]E[X]E[X] = sum of xP(X=x)x P(X = x)xP(X=x); the variance is EEE[(XXX - mean)^2] = E[X2]−(E[X])2E[X^{2}] - (E[X])^{2}E[X2]−(E[X])2. Linearity of expectation, E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y], holds without independence and is the most useful trick: write a count as a sum of indicator variables (1 if an event occurs, else 0), whose expectations are simply probabilities. Variance adds only for independent (or uncorrelated) variables.

    Standard distributions. Bernoulli (one trial with success probability p), binomial (number of successes in n trials: mean np, variance np(1 - p)), geometric (trials until the first success: mean 1p\frac{1}{p}p1​), Poisson (count of rare independent events: mean and variance both λ\lambda λ), exponential (waiting time with rate λ\lambda λ: mean 1λ\frac{1}{\lambda }λ1​ and memoryless, meaning the remaining wait does not depend on the time already waited), uniform and normal. The normal distribution matters because of the central limit theorem: the average of many independent draws is approximately normal, with standard deviation σn\frac{\sigma }{\sqrt{n}}n​σ​.

    Inequalities and limits. Markov: P(X≥a)≤E[X]aP(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}P(X≥a)≤aE[X]​ for non-negative XXX. Chebyshev: PPP(|XXX - mean| >= kσk \sigma kσ) <= 1k2\frac{1}{k^{2}}k21​. Hoeffding bounds how far an average of bounded variables strays from its mean; it justifies bandit algorithms. The law of large numbers says averages converge to the mean.

    Statistics. An estimator is unbiased if its expectation equals the true value; maximum likelihood chooses the parameter that makes the data most probable. A confidence interval for a mean is roughly the sample mean plus or minus 2 standard errors; a p-value is the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of a result at least this extreme (it is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true).

    Q2. Roll two fair dice. What is the probability that the sum is 5? That the sum is at least 10?

    Answer. There are 36 equally likely outcomes. Sum 5: (1,4), (2,3), (3,2), (4,1), so 436=19\frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9}364​=91​. Sum at least 10: (4,6), (5,5), (6,4), (5,6), (6,5), (6,6), so 636=16\frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}366​=61​.

    Q3. A disease affects 1% of people. A test is positive for 99% of people who have it and for 5% of people who do not. You test positive. What is the probability you have the disease?

    The problem: Bayes' rule turns P(positive given disease) into P(disease given positive). The surprise is that a good test can still give mostly false alarms when the disease is rare.

    Answer. P(disease and positive) = 0.01×0.99=0.00990.01 \times 0.99 = 0.00990.01×0.99=0.0099. P(no disease and positive) = 0.99×0.05=0.04950.99 \times 0.05 = 0.04950.99×0.05=0.0495. Out of 10,000 people, 100 are sick (99 test positive) and 9,900 are healthy (495 test positive), so only 99 of the 594 positives are sick.

    Q4. The Monty Hall problem, and two children. The problem (Monty Hall): a prize is behind one of three doors; you pick one; the host, who knows where the prize is, opens another door showing no prize and offers you a switch. Should you switch?

    Answer. Yes: switching wins with probability 23\frac{2}{3}32​. Your first pick is right with probability 13\frac{1}{3}31​ and stays right if you keep it. If your first pick was wrong (probability 23\frac{2}{3}32​), the host is forced to reveal the only other empty door, so switching gets the prize. The two-children problem: a family has two children and at least one is a boy; what is the probability both are boys? The equally likely cases are BB, BG, GB (GG excluded), so the answer is 13\frac{1}{3}31​. If instead you are told 'the older child is a boy', the cases are BB and BG, so 12\frac{1}{2}21​. The wording changes the information.

    Q7. Define expectation, variance and covariance; state the law of total expectation and total variance.

    Answer. E[X]E[X]E[X] is the probability-weighted average of the values. Var(XXX) = EEE[(X−E[X])2(X - E[X])^{2}(X−E[X])2] = E[X2]−E[X]2E[X^{2}] - E[X]^{2}E[X2]−E[X]2 measures spread. Cov(XXX, YYY) = EEE[XY] - EEE[XXX]E[Y]E[Y]E[Y] measures how they move together; correlation divides by both standard deviations to give a number between -1 and 1. Independent variables have zero covariance, but zero covariance does not imply independence (take XXX uniform on [-1, 1] and Y=X2Y = X^{2}Y=X2). Law of total expectation: E[X]=EE[X] = EE[X]=E[E[X∣Y]E[X \mid Y]E[X∣Y]]. Law of total variance: Var(XXX) = EEE[Var(XXX given YYY)] + Var(E[X∣Y]E[X \mid Y]E[X∣Y]), the average within-group variance plus the variance of the group means.

    Q8. How do you compute the covariance of two normal random variables?

    Answer. The covariance is not determined by the two marginal distributions; you need how they are related. In general Cov(X, YYY) = EEE[XY] - EEE[X]E[Y]E[Y]E[Y]. If X and YYY are jointly normal (a bivariate normal), Cov is the off-diagonal entry of the covariance matrix, equal to ρ\rho ρ times σX\sigma _{X}σX​ times σY\sigma _{Y}σY​; and for jointly normal variables only, zero covariance does imply independence. Say that you would ask whether they are jointly normal and what the relationship is.

    Q9. List the standard distributions with mean, variance and when they appear.

    Answer. Bernoulli(p): one trial; mean p, variance p(1 - p). Binomial(n, p): successes in n trials; mean np, variance np(1 - p). Geometric(p): number of trials until the first success; mean 1p\frac{1}{p}p1​; memoryless. Poisson(λ\lambda λ): count of rare independent events; mean and variance both λ\lambda λ; the limit of Binomial(n, λn\frac{\lambda }{n}nλ​). Uniform(a, bbb): mean a+b2\frac{a + b}{2}2a+b​, variance (b−a)212\frac{(b - a)^{2}}{12}12(b−a)2​. Normal(μ\mu μ, σ2\sigma ^{2}σ2): sums of many small independent effects; 68% within 1 standard deviation, 95% within 1.96, 99.7% within 3.

    Q10. Law of large numbers versus central limit theorem.

    Answer. For independent identically distributed variables with mean μ\mu μ and variance σ2\sigma ^{2}σ2, the law of large numbers says the sample average converges to μ\mu μ as n grows. The central limit theorem describes how: the average minus μ\mu μ, scaled by nσ\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sigma }σn​​, is approximately standard normal, so the error of a sample mean shrinks like σn\frac{\sigma }{\sqrt{n}}n​σ​. This is why Monte Carlo error falls like 1 / sqrt(number of samples), and why you need 100 times more samples for 10 times more accuracy. The central limit theorem needs finite variance; with heavy tails (as in some financial returns) convergence is slow or fails.

    Answer. Markov: for a non-negative XXX and a>0a > 0a>0, P(X≥a)≤E[X]aP(X \ge a) \le \frac{E[X]}{a}P(X≥a)≤aE[X]​. If the mean waiting time is 10 minutes, at most 20% of waits exceed 50 minutes. Chebyshev: PPP(|X−μX - \mu X−μ| >= kσk \sigma kσ) <= 1k2\frac{1}{k^{2}}k21​, so at most 25% of values are more than 2 standard deviations from the mean, for any distribution with finite variance. They are weak but need no assumptions. Hoeffding's inequality (used in bandit algorithms): for nnn independent variables bounded in [0, 1], PPP(sample mean - true mean >= eps) <= exp⁡(−2nϵ2)\exp (-2 n \epsilon^{2})exp(−2nϵ2).

    Q12. Maximum likelihood estimation: derive the estimates for a Bernoulli and a normal sample.

    The problem: the maximum likelihood estimate picks the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable.

    Answer.     Q13. Hypothesis testing and confidence intervals: a worked case. A sample of 100 measurements has mean 52 and the standard deviation is known to be 10. Test whether the true mean is 50, and give a 95% confidence interval.

    Answer. The standard error is 10100=1\frac{10}{\sqrt{100}} = 1100​10​=1. The test statistic is z=52−501=2z = \frac{52 - 50}{1} = 2z=152−50​=2. The two-sided p-value is about 0.0455, the probability of a result at least this extreme if the true mean were 50; at the 5% level we reject. The 95% confidence interval is 52 plus or minus 1.96×11.96 \times 11.96×1, which is (50.04, 53.96). A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true; and a confidence interval's 95% refers to the procedure covering the truth in 95% of repeated experiments.

    2.2 Stochastic processes and queueing

    Concept primer

    A stochastic process is a random quantity evolving over time. The objects here appear directly in reinforcement learning, queueing and finance.

    Markov chains. A Markov chain jumps between states, and the next state depends only on the current one: PPP(next = jjj given current = iii) = P(i,j)P(i, j)P(i,j). The transition matrix PPP has rows that sum to 1. The distribution after n steps is the starting row vector times PnP^{n}Pn. A stationary distribution π\pi π satisfies πP=π\pi P = \pi πP=π (and its entries add to 1): once reached, it stays. An irreducible, aperiodic chain on finitely many states has a unique stationary distribution and converges to it from any start; πi\pi _{i}πi​ also equals the long-run fraction of time in state iii, and the expected return time to iii is 1πi\frac{1}{\pi _{i}}πi​1​. Expected hitting times solve a linear system: for the time hhh(iii) to reach a target, hhh(target) = 0 and h(i)=1h(i) = 1h(i)=1 + sum over jjj of P(i,j)h(j)P(i, j) h(j)P(i,j)h(j). Absorption probabilities solve a similar system without the "1 +".

    Random walks and martingales. A simple random walk adds +1 or -1 each step. After nnn steps its variance is nnn, so it spreads like n\sqrt{n}n​. Gambler's ruin asks for the chance a gambler starting with i reaches N before 0; for a fair game it is iN\frac{i}{N}Ni​. A martingale is a process whose expected next value equals its current value given the past; it models a fair game. The optional stopping theorem says that, under mild conditions, the expected value at a stopping time equals the starting value, which gives slick solutions: for the fair walk, SnS_{n}Sn​ and Sn2−nS_{n}^{2} - nSn2​−n are martingales, which yields both the ruin probability and the expected duration i (N - i).

    Poisson process and queues. Events arrive at random with rate λ\lambda λ; the gaps between events are independent exponential variables and the count in time ttt is Poisson with mean λt\lambda tλt. Splitting and merging Poisson streams gives Poisson streams. A queue with arrival rate λ\lambda λ and service rate μ\mu μ has utilization ρ=λμ\rho = \frac{\lambda }{\mu }ρ=μλ​; it is stable only when ρ<1\rho < 1ρ<1. For the MM1\frac{\frac{M}{M}}{1}1MM​​ queue (Poisson arrivals, exponential service, one server) the average number in the system is ρ1−ρ\frac{\rho }{1 - \rho }1−ρρ​ and the average time in the system is 1μ−λ\frac{1}{\mu - \lambda }μ−λ1​; the delay blows up as ρ\rho ρ approaches 1. Little's law, L=λWL = \lambda WL=λW, holds for any stable system: average number equals arrival rate times average time spent.

    Continuous time. Brownian motion is the limit of scaled random walks: continuous paths, independent Gaussian increments with variance equal to elapsed time. Ito's lemma is the chain rule for such processes, with an extra second-order term because (dW)2=dt(dW)^{2} = dt(dW)2=dt. It is the tool behind Black-Scholes (Part 4).

    This is the core of Dr. Bodas's area (stochastic modelling, queueing theory, Markov decision processes). Learn it well.

    Q1. What is a Markov chain? Find the long-run fraction of sunny days.

    The problem: a Markov chain moves between states, and the next state depends only on the current state (not on the past). It is described by a table of transition probabilities. Weather example: after a sunny day, it is sunny with probability 0.8 and rainy with probability 0.2; after a rainy day it is sunny with probability 0.4 and rainy with probability 0.6.

    Answer. The long-run (stationary) distribution π\pi π satisfies π=πP\pi = \pi Pπ=πP and sums to 1. Write π=(s,r)\pi = (s, r)π=(s,r). Balance of flow between the states: s×0.2=r×0.4s \times 0.2 = r \times 0.4s×0.2=r×0.4, so sss = 2r, and s+r=1s + r = 1s+r=1 gives s=23s = \frac{2}{3}s=32​, r=13r = \frac{1}{3}r=31​. So two thirds of days are sunny in the long run. This chain is irreducible (every state reachable from every other) and aperiodic, so the distribution after many steps converges to π\pi π from any start. See solving a Markov chain for the general method.

    Q2. Classify states: what are recurrent, transient, periodic, and when does a chain converge to its stationary distribution?

    Answer. A state is recurrent if, starting there, the chain returns with probability 1 (positive recurrent if the expected return time is finite), and transient if there is a positive chance it never returns. A chain is irreducible if all states communicate. A state has period d if returns can only happen at times that are multiples of d. A finite irreducible chain is positive recurrent and has a unique stationary distribution; if it is also aperiodic, it converges to that distribution from any starting state. Example of failure: a chain that alternates deterministically between two states has period 2; it has a stationary distribution (12,12)(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})(21​,21​) but never converges to it. Example of transient states: a gambler's wealth, once at 0 or at the target, is absorbed; all other states are transient.

    Q3. Gambler's ruin. A gambler starts with i rupees and repeatedly bets 1 rupee on a fair coin until reaching 0 (ruin) or N (target). What is the probability of reaching N, and the expected number of bets? What if the coin wins with probability 0.6?

    Q5. Poisson process. Calls arrive at a rate of 6 per hour. (a) What is the probability of no call in 20 minutes? (b) What is the expected time until the third call?     The problem: a Poisson process models events that occur independently at a constant average rate; the number of events in a period is Poisson-distributed and the waiting time between events is exponentially distributed.

    Answer. (a) The number of calls in 20 minutes (13\frac{1}{3}31​ hour) has mean 6×13=26 \times \frac{1}{3} = 26×31​=2, so P(none) = e−2=0.135e^{-2} = 0.135e−2=0.135. Thinning works the other way: keeping each event with probability p gives a Poisson process of rate p times the original.

    Q6. Exponential distribution: why is it memoryless, and what is the minimum of two independent exponentials?

    Answer.     2.3 Linear algebra, calculus, optimization and game theory

    Concept primer

    These are the mathematical tools you need for machine learning and optimization.

    Linear algebra. A matrix A represents a linear map. The rank is the number of independent rows (or columns); Ax=bA x = bAx=b has a solution when bbb is in the column space, and it is unique when the columns are independent. An eigenvector vvv of a square matrix satisfies Av=λvA v = \lambda vAv=λv: the map only stretches it. For a symmetric matrix the eigenvalues are real and the eigenvectors can be chosen orthogonal, so A=QDQTA = Q D Q^{T}A=QDQT. A symmetric matrix is positive semidefinite if all eigenvalues are at least 0, equivalently xTAx≥0x^{T} A x \ge 0xTAx≥0 for all xxx; covariance matrices have this property. The singular value decomposition writes any matrix as USVTU S V^{T}USVT and underlies PCA and low-rank approximation. Least squares chooses www to minimize the squared error ||yyy - XXX www||^2; setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equations XTXw=XTyX^{T} X w = X^{T} yXTXw=XTy.

    Calculus and optimization. The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase; at an unconstrained minimum it is zero. The Hessian (matrix of second derivatives) tells curvature: positive definite means a local minimum. A function is convex if the segment between two points on its graph lies above the graph; for convex functions every local minimum is global, which is why convex problems are easy. Gradient descent steps against the gradient with step size η\eta η; it converges for suitable η\eta η (below 2L\frac{2}{L}L2​ for a function whose gradient is L-Lipschitz). Constrained problems: Lagrange multipliers turn "minimize f subject to g=0g = 0g=0" into finding where the gradient of f is parallel to the gradient of ggg; the KKT conditions extend this to inequality constraints.

    Game theory. A game has players, strategies and payoffs. A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies where nobody gains by changing their own strategy alone. A mixed strategy randomizes; in a mixed equilibrium each player makes the other indifferent between the strategies they randomize over, which is how you solve small games. In a zero-sum game the minimax theorem says the best guaranteed outcomes of the two players coincide, and the value can be found by linear programming.

    Q1. What are eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and why do they matter?

    Q2. What is rank, and when is a linear system Ax=bA x = bAx=b solvable?

    Answer. The rank of a matrix is the number of linearly independent columns (equal to the number of independent rows). Ax=bA x = bAx=b has a solution if and only if bbb lies in the column space of A, equivalently rank(A) = rank of the augmented matrix [A | bbb].     Q9. Game theory: what is a Nash equilibrium? Find it in the prisoner's dilemma and in matching pennies.

    The problem: a game specifies players, their available actions and payoffs. A Nash equilibrium is a combination of strategies in which no player can gain by changing only their own strategy.

    Answer.     Q10. Why is bidding your true value optimal in a second-price auction?

    Q11. What is the 'power of two choices'?
